Course design

How the two days fit together

Both days teach one habit, Mask → Ask → Check, and practice it on one set of fictional TIYA-style documents. Participants spend most of the day at the keyboard; instruction comes in short bursts between exercises.

1 · Mask

Strip or replace sensitive data before anything goes into Copilot.

  • Names → roles
  • Numbers → placeholders
  • Pricing, CUI, PHI never go in
2 · Ask

Prompt with Microsoft's four parts for Copilot.

  • Goal: what you want
  • Context: why, for whom
  • Source: what to use
  • Expectations: format, length, tone
3 · Check

A person verifies before anything is used or sent.

  • Facts, numbers, names
  • Policy and tone
  • "Would I sign this?"

Design principles

  • Keyboard time first. Every block except the policy block has participants typing within 15 minutes of its start. Day 1 has about 5.5 hours hands-on; Day 2 has 3.5 hours of building, plus design and red-team exercises.
  • One habit, repeated. Mask → Ask → Check appears on every exercise card, so by 4:30 it is reflexive and the closing assessment tests it directly.
  • One sample world. The same fictional work orders, SOPs, and building names run through both days. Participants spend their attention on the AI technique, not on learning new material each hour. Day 2 introduces its own files (the sandbox SOPs and the work order export), so a Day 2 cohort that took Day 1 months earlier doesn't need to remember the Day 1 samples.
  • Real work, masked. The role lab and the Day 2 build invite participants to bring a real task, but only after masking it. Instructors check masking before anyone pastes.
  • AI drafts, people decide. No exercise ends with AI output used as-is. Each one ends with a Check step and a share-out about what had to be fixed.

Instructor roles

  • Djalali (lead): delivers instruction blocks, runs live demos, leads debriefs, keeps time.
  • Rhodeen (co-instructor): runs the morning login check, circulates during labs, owns the remote channel, collects prompts for the "prompt wall", flags masking problems quietly to individuals. Swap lead on the leadership block (Day 1) and the governance block (Day 2) if you want a change of voice.

Remote participants

  • Join the Teams meeting with camera on during exercises if possible. Rhodeen watches chat throughout and voices remote questions at each debrief.
  • Remote participants get the digital workbook and the materials folder link the day before.
  • During labs, pair remote participants with each other in a Teams breakout room, or with an in-room partner on a laptop call.
  • Share-outs: remote participants paste their prompt and result into chat; the lead reads one aloud each debrief.

Materials

  • Participant workbook, one printed copy each plus the digital version (prompts are copy-ready).
  • Slide outlines, built into PowerPoint with GT branding.
  • Assessment kit: knowledge checks, exit tasks, rubrics, certificate criteria, evaluation form.
  • Materials folder (materials/): sample SOPs as Word files and a work order export as CSV. Upload these to a training SharePoint site before Day 2.
  • Printed: Mask/Ask/Check table tents, "Can I paste this?" quick-fire cards, red-team attack cards, certificates.

Open items

Decisions for the program owner

  1. Syllabus wording on tools. The Day 1 outline says "tools like ChatGPT". The course is built on Microsoft 365 Copilot. Suggest "tools like Microsoft 365 Copilot" so participants don't expect ChatGPT access.
  2. Day 2 outcomes vs. agent builder. Outcome 2 promises autonomous agents and the outline lists event triggers. The agent builder makes on-demand agents; triggers and autonomous agents need Copilot Studio or Power Automate. This guide teaches triggers as a concept with an instructor demo and a design exercise. Either soften the outcome ("design … and deploy agents, and design event-triggered automations") or provide a Copilot Studio environment for the demo.
  3. Day 2 closing. The Day 2 outline ends at 4:30 with no closing or assessment. This guide uses the last 10 minutes of the governance block for the exit checklist and certificates. If the day runs to 5:00, move them to a 4:30–5:00 closing block.
  4. Day 2 headers. The Day 2 outline reuses Day 1's session titles. Suggested: Morning "Agent Foundations & First Builds"; Afternoon "Deploy, Trigger & Govern".
  5. Certificate criteria. The assessment kit proposes: full attendance (up to 30 minutes excused) plus a completed exit task. Confirm, or set a stricter bar.

Say

Map

Story

"A contracts assistant at a company like ours gets a vendor's quote for a chiller replacement. They want a quick comparison against last year's price, so they paste both quotes into a free AI tool on their phone. The comparison is great. But two companies' pricing has now left the building, into a service the company doesn't control, under terms nobody read. Nothing bad has to happen next for this to be a problem. It already is one." (Fictional; tell it as a hypothetical.)

Categories
  • PII: anything that identifies a person: names with other details, phone numbers, home addresses, employee IDs, DoD ID numbers, SSNs.
  • PHI: health information tied to a person: injuries, medical restrictions, workers' comp details.
  • CUI: government information marked or designated as Controlled Unclassified Information, including some facility, security, and infrastructure details. If you don't know whether something is CUI, treat it as CUI and ask.
  • Pricing: TIYA's rates, cost data, bids, proposals, and any vendor's quote.
Say

"Copilot signed in with your TIYA account has enterprise data protection. Microsoft doesn't use your prompts to train its models. That's real and it matters. It does not change the rules for CUI, PHI, or pricing. Those rules come from TIYA's policy and its contracts, not from the tool."

Demo

Ask Copilot: Which FAR clause covers limitations on subcontracting for small business set-asides? Quote the key paragraph. Then open acquisition.gov on screen and compare the clause number and wording. Whether Copilot gets it right or not, the point lands: you check against the source, every time it matters.

Masking key

Sample B contains 11 items to mask or remove: 3 names, 1 phone number, 1 email address, 1 injury detail (PHI, remove entirely), 1 contract number, 2 dollar amounts (pricing, remove), 1 vendor name, 1 gate access code (remove entirely). Most people miss the access code and the vendor name. The answer key is in the assessment kit.

Quick-fire key
  1. Generic HVAC troubleshooting question: green.
  2. Meeting notes listing attendees by name: amber; replace names with roles.
  3. Subcontractor's bid spreadsheet: red.
  4. Published FAR clause text: green.
  5. Email about an employee's medical restriction: red.
  6. A SOW excerpt marked CUI: red.
  7. Your own draft newsletter article: green.
  8. Overtime hours by named employee: amber; use Employee A, B, C, or aggregate.

Ladder
  1. Prompt: you ask, it answers, it forgets.
  2. Saved prompt: you reuse good instructions.
  3. Agent: standing instructions, its own knowledge, and tools, for one defined job. You start each conversation. Agent builder lives here.
  4. Triggered agent: starts itself when something happens, like a new email or a scheduled time.
  5. Autonomous agent: plans and takes multi-step actions in other systems, with people approving at checkpoints. Copilot Studio territory.

"Each rung up gives the agent more independence, so each rung needs more design, more testing, and more governance."

Anatomy
  • Purpose: one job, one audience. Agent builder: name and description.
  • Instructions: a standing prompt. Agent builder: yes.
  • Knowledge: what it reads: SharePoint, files, web. Agent builder: yes.
  • Tools: what it can do beyond text. Agent builder: some built-in capabilities, such as code interpreter and image generation. Connections to other systems: Copilot Studio.
  • Memory: what it remembers. Agent builder: the current conversation. Long-term memory varies by tenant.
  • Triggers: what starts it. Agent builder: a person. Events and schedules: Copilot Studio or Power Automate.
  • Guardrails: what it must not do. Agent builder: instructions, plus the permissions of its knowledge.
Architectures
  • Knowledge Q&A: answers from a defined set of documents (the SOP Navigator).
  • Task assistant: walks a person through a structured task with a fixed output format.
  • Multi-tool: combines knowledge with tools, such as analyzing an uploaded spreadsheet (the Work Order Analyst).
  • Orchestrated or multi-agent: one agent hands work to others, often triggered by events. Mentioned for awareness; not built today.
Sort key

The answer key for "Agent or not?" is in the assessment kit. Expect debate on items 4 and 9; that debate is the point.

Triggers

"Everything you built this morning waits for someone to ask. A triggered agent starts itself: a new email arrives in the work order mailbox, a file lands in a SharePoint folder, it's 7 AM Monday. In Microsoft's stack, that's Copilot Studio or Power Automate. The design thinking is the same, and that's what we'll practice."

Demo

If a Copilot Studio or Power Automate environment is available, show a flow that starts on a new email to a shared mailbox, summarizes it, and posts to a Teams channel for a person to approve. Otherwise, walk through the screenshots captured during prep. Stress the approval step: the person stays in the loop.

Memory

"Assume the agent remembers nothing between conversations. If it needs context, design it to ask for it, or put that context in a document it reads as knowledge."

Publishing
  • The agent can only show a user what that user can already open. Sharing an agent doesn't share its knowledge, so check that your audience can open the sources.
  • That cuts both ways: an agent built on a folder with loose permissions will surface whatever is in it to everyone who can open it.
  • Share with named people first. Organization-wide availability goes through TIYA's review (governance block).
  • Naming: TIYA · [Team] · [Job], for example TIYA · FM · SOP Navigator. The description says what it does, what it doesn't, and who owns it.

Say

"An instruction like 'never reveal personal information' is a request, not a lock. A clever question can get around it. The real lock is what the agent can read. If it can't see it, it can't leak it. So the most important governance decision is what you put in the knowledge."

Never as knowledge

HR and personnel files, medical or injury records, pricing and cost data, bid documents, CUI, security details such as access codes or camera locations, and any folder whose permissions you haven't checked.

Lifecycle

Propose → Build → Test → Review → Share → Monitor → Re-test → Retire. Re-test when the knowledge changes, when the instructions change, and periodically anyway: Microsoft updates the underlying models, and behavior can shift without anyone touching the agent.

Debrief
  1. Which attack worked on your agent? What fixed it, and is the fix a lock or a request?
  2. What tier is your agent? Did your partner agree?
  3. Who at TIYA should own the list of shared agents?
